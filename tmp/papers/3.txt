Abstract
Cross-view isolated sign language recognition (CV-ISLR)
addresses the challenge of identifying isolated signs from
viewpoints unseen during training, a problem aggravated by
the scarcity of multi-view data in existing benchmarks. To
bridge this gap, we introduce a novel two-stage framework
comprising View Synthesis and Contrastive Multi-task View-
Semantics Recognition. In the View Synthesis stage, we sim-
ulate unseen viewpoints by extracting 3D keypoints from the
front-view training dataset and synthesizing common-view
2D skeleton sequences with virtual camera rotation, which
enriches view diversity without the cost of multi-camera
setups. However, direct training on these synthetic sam-
ples leads to limited improvement, as viewpoint-specific
and semantics-specific features remain entangled. To over-
come this drawback, we present a Contrastive Multi-task
View-Semantics Recognition (CMVSR) module that disentan-
gles viewpoint-dependent features from sign semantics. In
this way, CMVSR obtains view-invariant representations of
the sign video, leading to robust recognition performance
against various camera viewpoints. We evaluate our ap-
proach on the MM-WLAuslan dataset, the first benchmark
for CV-ISLR, and on our extended protocol MTV-Test that
includes additional multi-view data captured in the wild.
Experimental results demonstrate that our method not only
improves the accuracy of front-view skeleton-based isolated
sign language recognition, but also exhibits superior gener-
alization to novel viewpoints. The project page is publicly
available at CV-ISLR-MTV /gtb.

A promising strategy for CV-ISLR involves synthesizing
multi-view data from front-view videos, allowing models
to learn from a more diverse set of viewpoints. Neverthe-
less, we observe that directly training on synthetic multi-
view samples yields limited gains, as models tend to encode
viewpoint-dependent cues rather than view-invariant repre-
sentations. To overcome these challenges, we propose a
two-stage framework composed of View Synthesis and Con-
trastive Multi-task View-Semantics Recognition.
In the View Synthesis stage, we extract 3D whole-body
keypoints from front-view training data using methods such
as SMPL-X-based models [5, 30, 63] or image-based regres-
sion techniques [20, 70]. Instead of synthesizing multi-view
RGB videos, we focus on 2D skeleton sequences that are
more efficient to process and less sensitive to background and
lighting variations [21]. Specifically, we rotate a virtual cam-
era to diverse yaw and pitch angles, and apply perspective
transformations to project the 3D keypoints into their corre-
sponding 2D skeletons. This strategy significantly enriches
the training data with various viewing directions without the
need for expensive multi-camera setups.
In the Contrastive Multi-task View-Semantics Recogni-
tion stage, our goal is to train robust models on the syn-
thesized multi-view sign data by effectively disentangling
viewpoint-specific features from semantics-specific features.
First, we utilize a cross-attention mechanism [51] to separate
viewpoint and semantic information from entangled sign
language embeddings. Concretely, we construct triplets of
skeletal samples: two sequences that share identical sign
semantics but differ in their viewpoints, paired with a third
sequence that maintains the same viewpoint while represent-
ing distinct semantics. Then, we employ a multi-task learn-
ing framework that simultaneously predicts sign semantics
and viewpoints. This design ensures that viewpoint features
are treated as a distinct learning objective instead of being
inadvertently entangled in the sign classification pathway. Fi-
nally, we apply contrastive learning objectives [35] that align
embeddings of similar viewpoints or semantics while sepa-
rating mismatched ones. Our approach encourages the model
to learn truly view-invariant sign representations, thereby
improving generalization to unseen camera viewpoints.
To validate our idea, we evaluate our method on the MM-
WLAuslan dataset [39], the first benchmark specifically de-
signed for CV-ISLR. However, we note that the original test
scenarios in MM-WLAuslan lack the sample diversity to
reflect real-world complexity. To address this, as shown in
Figure 1 (b), we extend the test set with newly recorded
videos, named MTV-Test, covering a wide range of cam-
era viewpoints, devices, and environmental contexts. Our
method achieves state-of-the-art performance in front-view
skeleton-based ISLR and consistently excels in challenging
non-front-view scenarios, demonstrating the effectiveness
of our view synthesis and view-semantics disentanglement
approach. In summary, our key contributions are as follows:
• We introduce a skeleton-based method to synthesize multi-
view data from front-view sign videos, mitigating the need
for extensive manual data collection.
• We design a multi-task disentanglement module that simul-
taneously predicts sign semantics and viewpoints, guided
by dual contrastive learning objectives to enhance cross-
view generalization.
• We provide a more diverse and realisticMTV-Testset that
extends the MM-WLAuslan dataset with additional cam-
era viewpoints and background complexities, offering a
stronger evaluation protocol for future CV-ISLR studies.
2. Related Work
2.1. Isolated Sign Language Recognition Datasets
A variety of datasets have been developed to advance ISLR
research and industrial applications. However, many exist-
ing collections are constrained by factors such as limited
gloss vocabulary, insufficient contextual depth, or restricted
camera viewpoints. For instance, the Purdue RVL-SLLL
dataset [ 31] is methodologically sound under controlled
conditions, yet it only contains 39 signs, greatly limiting
its utility for large-scale sign recognition. Similarly, al-
though the ASLLVD dataset [3] offers an extensive lexicon
of 3,000 glosses, it relies on a single-camera perspective
without depth information, which is crucial for capturing
three-dimensional sign movements. WLASL [27] and MS-
ASL [22] both expand in terms of signer variety and sign
categories but still rely on single-view videos without depth
data, missing essential spatial details for accurate sign inter-
pretation. Meanwhile, datasets such as CSL 500 [ 17] and
DGS Kinect 40 [1] include depth information but cover rel-
atively few glosses, thus restricting their applicability for
real-world tasks.
By contrast, MM-WLAuslan [39] is a more comprehen-
sive dataset for ISLR. It encompasses 3,215 unique signs per-
formed by 73 signers, with each sign captured from four dis-
tinct viewpoints alongside depth data, thereby greatly enrich-
ing both sample diversity and practical usefulness. By pro-
viding these multiple camera viewpoints, MM-WLAuslan is
currently the only dataset explicitly designed for cross-view
ISLR. Furthermore, to better evaluate in-the-wild conditions,
we introduce an extendedMTV-Testset, which covers a wider
range of camera perspectives and environmental scenarios.

2.2. Isolated Sign Language Recognition Methods
ISLR aims to identify the gloss labels of short-term
videos [26]. Prior research can be broadly grouped into
three categories based on the input modality: pixel-based,
skeleton-based, and multi-modal approaches. Specifically,
pixel-based methods build upon CNN-based action recogni-
tion, where early works [7, 8, 25] first employ convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) to extract frame-level features, and
then apply temporal modeling through recurrent architec-
tures. More recent efforts explore 3D CNN variants such
as C3D [ 18, 23, 48, 56] and I3D [ 6], which jointly cap-
ture spatiotemporal cues [ 9, 14, 22, 27, 29]. In contrast,
skeleton-based approaches emphasize key body joints and
therefore exhibit greater robustness to background clutter,
illumination changes, and partial occlusions [11, 44, 58, 62].
ST-GCN [62] pioneers the use of spatiotemporal graph con-
volutions in action recognition, inspiring various ISLR ex-
tensions [16, 27, 50, 66], including Transformer-based mod-
els [4, 15, 26, 51]. Meanwhile, multi-modal approaches,
which integrate multiple data streams such as pixel, skeleton,
and depth information, have demonstrated notable perfor-
mance gains. For example, Zuo et al. [71] employ S3D to
extract RGB and pose heatmap features, achieving improved
WLASL [27] recognition, while Jiang et al. [19] incorporate
depth information to surpass 99% accuracy on AUTSL [46].
However, due to the limited diversity in training view-
points, most of these methods are predominantly trained on
front-view data, resulting in significant performance drops in
CV-ISLR. In contrast to such approaches, our framework em-
ploys synthesized multi-view data and applies disentangle-
ment of viewpoint and sign semantics, thereby substantially
improving robustness across various viewpoints.
2.3. Cross-View Action Recognition
It has been argued [34] that many action recognition (AR)
techniques can be adapted for sign language tasks. In the
pursuit of robust real-world systems, whether for ISLR or
broader AR, multi-view and multi-modal learning is consid-
ered essential [32]. Recent AR research explores a variety of
strategies for handling multi-view data [24], including dictio-
nary learning [10], adaptive neural networks [64], convolu-
tional pipelines [13], and attention-based mechanisms [69].
Vision Transformer models have also proven effective in
multi-view contexts [ 45, 68]. Other contemporary stud-
ies [54, 67] develop robust view-invariant representations for
downstream tasks, while DA-Net [52] fuses view-specific
and view-agnostic modules to achieve better predictive per-
formance. Methods such as feature factorization [ 37] and
cascaded residual autoencoders [49] address RGB-D recog-
nition and incomplete-view classification, respectively.
Unlike these AR-oriented solutions, our work specifically
targets CV-ISLR by leveraging synthesized multi-view train-
ing data. Moreover, we are the first to disentangle viewpoints
and semantics within a sign language recognition framework,
providing a promising avenue for effective CV-ISLR.
3. Cross-View Setup in MM-WLAuslan and the
Proposed MTV Test Set
3.1. MM-WLAuslan Setup
MM-WLAuslan [39] is currently the only dataset explicitly
designed for Cross-View Isolated Sign Language Recogni-
tion (CV-ISLR). It contains over 282K RGB-D sign record-
ings of 3,215 frequently used Auslan glosses, captured from
four different viewpoints via Kinect and RealSense sensors.
In contrast to traditional ISLR datasets, often restricted to
a single viewpoint, MM-WLAuslan provides multiple test
subsets (Figure 1 (b)), which allow benchmarking under var-
ious constraints. However, these subsets mostly involve yaw
angles within ±30◦ and predominantly rely on controlled
sensor environments, limiting their ability to reflect broader
real-world complexity. Additionally, the use of RGB-D de-
vices does not fully emulate the ubiquitous consumer-grade
cameras found in daily-life scenarios.
3.2. Proposed MTV-Test Set
To address these gaps, we propose MTV-Test, a test set that
greatly expands the diversity of the environment, beyond
what MM-WLAuslan currently offers. We recruit 36 partic-
ipants, including Auslan experts, Deaf signers, and volun-

Table 1 summarizes key statistics of the MM-WLAuslan
splits alongside our new MTV-Testset. Compared with the
original subsets, MTV offers a wider range of camera views
(including vertical viewpoints), more diverse backgrounds,
and a greater number of test-only signers unseen during
training. While MM-WLAuslan mainly focuses on ±30◦
yaw angles, the upward and downward views included in
MTV significantly expand the spatial variability. Further-
more, MTV replaces specialized RGB-D sensors with widely
available consumer devices, capturing recording conditions
that better mirror practical deployment scenarios. These
improvements create a more challenging and comprehen-
sive cross-view ISLR benchmark, enabling robust model
evaluation in real-world settings.

Part-Aware Sign Encoder via PA-ST-GCN.To effectively
capture structured skeleton representations, we employ a
Part-Aware Spatial Temporal Graph Convolutional Network
(PA-ST-GCN) that partitions the skeleton into two compo-
nents: the upper body (Kb = 13 keypoints) and the two
hands (Kh = 42 keypoints). This division enables better
spatial-temporal feature extraction using ST-GCN [21, 62].
Given a sequence of 2D skeleton keypoints S = [Sbody,
Shands], where Sbody ∈ Rf ×13×2 denotes the upper body
pose sequence, Shands ∈ Rf ×42×2 represents pose sequence
of the two hands, and f is the total number of frames. Each
part is independently processed using ST-GCN:
Fbody = ST-GCN(Sbody, Ab), (3)
Fhands = ST-GCN(Shands, Ah), (4)
where Ab and Ah denote the learned adjacency matrices
for the body and hands. The extracted features from both
parts are concatenated and then projected into a unified latent
space using a multi-layer perceptron (MLP):
Fskeleton = MLP(Concat(Fbody, Fhands)). (5)
The hierarchical feature extraction process ensures that both
upper-body motion and fine-grained hand movements are
effectively captured in a compact representation.

In the Part-Aware ST-GCN, each part is processed through
3 ST-GCN blocks [62], where the adjacency matrices are
initially constructed based on human anatomy and further
optimized during training. In the Cross-Attention module,
the view token and semantic tokens are initialized with a
feature dimension of 1024, and we employ 2 layers of 8-
head cross-attention to disentangle viewpoint-specific and
semantics-specific representations. By default, the view
token consists of a single learnable embedding, while the
semantic tokens are initialized as 64 learnable embeddings,
allowing the model to capture diverse sign-related features
effectively. The model is trained with a batch size of 128
for 50 epochs, using the ADAM optimizer with an initial
learning rate of 1 × 10−4, scheduled by CosineAnnealingLR.
Additionally, our proposed data augmentation techniques, in-
cluding viewpoint perturbation and temporal distortion, are
applied during training to improve robustness. By default,
the common viewpoints are set to seven predefined view-
points, as illustrated in Figure 2. The synthesized multi-view
data generated from these viewpoints are then used to further
train CMVSR.
5.4. Comparison with the State-of-the-art Methods
Table 2 compares our proposed CMVSR against various
state-of-the-art methods, including pixel-based, skeleton-
based, and multi-modal approaches, under both front-view
and cross-view settings. From the front-view scenario, we
observe that all methods perform relatively well, thanks to
the more constrained viewpoint. Nevertheless, skeleton-
based approaches (e.g., STC-SLR [ 66], DSTA-SLR [16],
KVNet-K [71]) demonstrate stronger resilience in complex
backgrounds (ITW, SYN), where pixel-based methods de-
grade under clutter or lighting variations. Our CMVSR
yields the highest accuracy among skeleton-based models,
nearly matching multi-modal performance, underscoring the
efficacy of our view-sign disentanglement strategy.
When the test viewpoint shifts beyond the frontal view
(MV), all models experience noticeable drops in accu-
racy. Pixel-based methods, in particular, exhibit significant
performance degradation, reflecting their heavier reliance
on viewpoint-dependent features. By contrast, CMVSR
achieves marked gains over existing skeleton-based tech-
niques, suggesting that synthesizing diverse viewpoints and
applying contrastive multi-task learning effectively enhance
cross-view robustness. Overall, our proposed CMVSR main-
tains a more consistent performance between frontal and
non-frontal settings, confirming its ability to handle real-
world variations in camera placement.

6. Conclusion
In this paper, we investigate the critical challenge of Cross-
View-ISLR, wherein limited multi-view training data and
viewpoint mismatches often hinder model performance in
real-world applications. To mitigate these issues, we propose
a two-stage approach: View Synthesis, which enriches train-
ing data via virtual 3D skeleton rotations, and Contrastive
Multi-task View-Semantics Recognition, which disentangles
viewpoint cues and semantic cues from sign embeddings.
Extensive experiments on MM-WLAuslan and our newMTV-
Test set demonstrate our method’s robustness against diverse
camera viewpoints and complex backgrounds, outperform-
ing pixel-based and skeleton-based CV-ISLR baselines. Our
work paves the way for more reliable and in-the-wild ISLR
solutions to benefit deaf and hard-of-hearing communities
in real-world scenarios.
